embedgemma2_tok vs Hugging Face tokenizers 0.23.2 (Python), Apple M5 Pro, 2026-10-16.

Measured on this tree (branch of commit 5229a1c plus the embedgemma2_tok commits), on a
SHARED, HEAVILY LOADED machine (load average 110 to 135 on 18 cores during the runs; other
sessions were building and benchmarking). Read the minima, not the medians, and treat the
spread as the result: a round's number moved by up to 5x between rounds of one run.

Command (12 interleaved rounds, the lane order reversed on odd rounds, every lane a fresh
process; each lane's number for a round is the fastest of 7 passes; load: of 5 loads):

  ~/.venvs/ml-2026-10/bin/python bench/embedgemma2_tok_hf.py corpus target/embedgemma2_tok_bench_corpus.bin
  EMBEDGEMMA2_SNAPSHOT=<snapshot> BENCH_TOK_CORPUS=target/embedgemma2_tok_bench_corpus.bin BENCH_ITERS=7 \
    target/release/bench_paired --rounds 12 --out bench/results/embedgemma2_tok_m5pro.json \
    tessl=target/release/bench_embedgemma2_tok \
    hf_loop="~/.venvs/ml-2026-10/bin/python bench/embedgemma2_tok_hf.py" \
    hf_batch:BENCH_TOK_HF_MODE=batch="~/.venvs/ml-2026-10/bin/python bench/embedgemma2_tok_hf.py"
  uv run --no-project --with matplotlib python bench/results/embedgemma2_tok_plot.py bench/results

(`~` written out as an absolute path in the real command: bench_paired splits on whitespace and
does not expand it.) The corpus is the file the first command writes (2701 records, 770900 bytes,
seeded). Both lanes read it, tokenize every text of a workload alone with special tokens, and
return the FNV-1a hash of all ids; the hashes are equal on every workload
(embedgemma2_tok_m5pro_tessl_lane.json vs embedgemma2_tok_m5pro_hf_lane.json: 2003484812,
641024712, 337202411, 1401142627), and the plot refuses to draw otherwise.

Lanes:
  tessl     bench_embedgemma2_tok: Tokenizer::encode_raw(text, true), one thread
  hf_loop   Tokenizer.encode(text) in a Python loop, one text at a time (the like-for-like lane)
  hf_batch  Tokenizer.encode_batch(texts): the library's own rayon parallelism on all cores
            (a reference for what the library gives when it may use the machine; tessl's
            encode_batch is sequential, so it is not compared against this one like for like)

Best round (min over 12 rounds of each round's min of 7 passes), ms, and tokens per second:

  short_queries    25097      6.621       17.198        9.559 |   3.79 M        1.46 M         2.63 M
  documents        83746     39.309       86.939       12.658 |   2.13 M        0.96 M         6.62 M
  multilingual     16016      4.021       14.689        4.237 |   3.98 M        1.09 M         3.78 M

Median over rounds of the per-round ratio hf_loop / tessl (from bench_paired; [lowest, highest]
round): short_queries 2.66 [2.10, 7.13], documents 2.36 [0.83, 8.17], multilingual 3.49
[2.83, 22.3], long_100k 3.56 [1.22, 16.5], load 1.30 [0.97, 1.99]. A spread that straddles
1.0 is not a result: documents and load do, in their lowest round.

What the numbers support:
  * One tessl thread encodes 2.2x to 3.7x the tokens per second of one tokenizers thread at the
    best round on all four workloads, and the median ratio is above 2.3 on all four; the
    load-straddling rounds are the noise of a loaded host, not a loss.
  * Against tokenizers' all-core encode_batch, tessl's one thread is ahead on short_queries
    (1.4x) and long_100k (3.3x: one 100k-character word cannot be split across cores), level on
    multilingual (1.05x, inside the noise) and behind on documents (0.32x: 200 independent
    documents parallelise).
  * Load: tessl 482 ms against 645 to 682 ms at the best round (1.3x to 1.4x); the medians
    (2.5 s against 3.0 to 3.4 s) are dominated by the host's load. On a quiet moment during
    development one load measured 379 ms (216 ms strict JSON parse, 147 ms building the tables).
  * Not claimed: any number on an idle machine, any comparison with another tokenizer, or that
    the ratios hold for text unlike this corpus. The corpus is synthetic (word lists, a few
    scripts, code lines), not a sample of a real workload.
